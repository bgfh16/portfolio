# Nigeria's Agro-Ecological Zones

Nigeria's climate changes steadily from the wet coast in the south to the dry north. Farmers and researchers group the country into agro-ecological zones. Each zone has its own rainfall, soils and typical crops, so the zone you farm in decides which crops suit you and when to plant them. Sources group the zones slightly differently. This document uses the commonly used list below.

## Rainfall pattern

- Yearly rainfall ranges from about 250-500 mm in the far northeast to about 3,000 mm near the coast.
- North of about latitude 9°N there is one rainy season (unimodal). Between about 4°N and 9°N there are two rainy seasons (bimodal).
- The dry season lasts from 3 to 8 months depending on the zone, and the rain is often erratic from year to year.

## The zones, from north to south

| Zone | Rainfall (mm per year) | Main crops |
|---|---|---|
| Sahel savanna | 250-500 | Millet, sorghum |
| Sudan savanna | 500-900 | Millet, sorghum, groundnut |
| Northern Guinea savanna | 900-1,200 | Maize, sorghum, soybean, cotton |
| Southern Guinea savanna | 1,200-1,500 | Yam, maize, sorghum, soybean, sesame |
| Derived savanna | 1,500-2,000 | Oil palm, yam, maize |
| Humid forest | 2,000-3,000 | Cocoa, oil palm |
| Mid-high altitude (Jos Plateau and other highlands) | 1,100-1,500 | Maize, potato, vegetables |

Rainfall and crop figures above come from one reference (OFRA, 2017). Other sources give slightly different averages, for example about 1,050 mm for the Guinea savanna and about 650 mm for the Sudan savanna, because they measure different places and years. Use the figures as a guide, not as exact limits.

The coastal mangrove and freshwater swamp areas receive up to about 3,000 mm of rain. The soil there is waterlogged, and swamp rice is grown only where the land is stable and not salty.

## Soils

- Most Nigerian soils are strongly leached. They are acidic, low in organic matter and low in nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium.
- Because the soil structure is fragile and low in nutrients, it is easily eroded.
- Guinea and Sudan savanna soils are often shallow and low in organic matter, and some have hard iron layers.
- Soils in the humid forest and derived savanna are generally deeper, sandy loam over sandy clay.

## What this means for farmers

- Choose crops that match your zone's rainfall and length of rainy season.
- Because the soils are low in nutrients, fertiliser, manure or compost usually pays off.
- See the documents on crops by zone, planting calendars and states by zone for more.

# Which Crops Suit Each Zone in Nigeria

This document links Nigeria's zones to the crops that are most commonly grown in them. The crops listed are the main ones named by research sources, not every crop that can grow there. See the zones document for rainfall and soils.

## Main crops by zone

| Zone | Main crops |
|---|---|
| Sahel savanna (far north, driest) | Millet, sorghum, with cowpea and groundnut where rain allows |
| Sudan savanna | Millet, sorghum, groundnut, cowpea, maize, cotton |
| Northern Guinea savanna | Maize, sorghum, soybean, cotton, cowpea, groundnut |
| Southern Guinea savanna | Yam, maize, sorghum, soybean, sesame |
| Derived savanna | Oil palm, yam, maize, cassava |
| Humid forest | Cocoa, oil palm, cassava, yam, cocoyam, plantain, maize |
| Mid-high altitude (Jos Plateau) | Maize, potato, vegetables |

## Cassava

Cassava is grown mainly in the wetter southern and middle parts of the country. IITA gives planting windows for four zones: the rainforest, the derived savanna, the southern Guinea savanna and the northern Guinea savanna. It does not give windows for the Sudan or Sahel zones.

## Maize

Maize is grown in almost every zone. It is a main crop in the Guinea savannas and the derived savanna, and farmers in the Sudan savanna also grow it, though millet and sorghum are more common there because they tolerate drought better. In the wetter south, maize is often planted twice in a year.

## Rice

Rice needs about 1,200-1,600 mm of well-spread rain, or a reliable water supply. It is therefore grown mostly in lowland areas near water, fadama (seasonally flooded land), swamps and irrigation schemes, and as upland rice where rainfall is enough. It appears in almost every zone, wherever water is available.

## Tomato

Tomato grows best in a cool, dry climate of about 21-25°C. NIHORT says the variety Roma VF suits dry-season planting in the north, where irrigation may be needed. When tomato is sown in the rainy season, the plants are staked at the peak of the rains. NIHORT lists some varieties for both north and south and others for the humid rainforest.

## Mixing crops on the same land

- In the north, cereals are often intercropped with legumes or other cereals.
- In the south, maize is usually intercropped with roots and tubers.
- In the Guinea and Sudan savannas, common double-cropping patterns include maize with cowpea, millet with maize, millet with cowpea and groundnut with maize. Sorghum in the Sudan savanna is mostly grown on its own.

# Planting Calendar and Rainy Seasons in Nigeria

The right planting time depends on when the rains become steady in your area. Planting too early risks a dry spell after the seeds sprout, and planting too late shortens the growing season. The dates below are guides, not fixed dates. Rain starts earlier in some years than in others, so always wait until the rains are properly established.

## When the rains come

- In the north (above about 9°N) there is one rainy season, and the farming year follows it.
- In the south (about 4°N to 9°N) there are two rainy seasons, so two crops a year are possible for some crops.
- Rain usually reaches the south first. In one season described by FAO, the first rains began in mid-March in the south and reached the north in mid to late April. In that season, planting of maize, rainfed rice, millet and sorghum started in May in the centre and north.
- The start of the rains differs from year to year, so follow local forecasts and your extension office as well.

## Planting windows by crop

| Crop | Zone | Planting time | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cassava | Rainforest | March to November | IITA |
| Cassava | Derived savanna | April to August | IITA |
| Cassava | Southern Guinea savanna | May to July | IITA |
| Cassava | Northern Guinea savanna | July to August | IITA |
| Maize | Southern Guinea savanna | Mid-May to second week of June | IITA |
| Maize | Northern Guinea savanna | Last week of June to first week of July | IITA |
| Maize | Sudan savanna | First to second week of July | IITA |
| Rice (rain-fed) | Forest zone | Mid-March to mid-April, after 2-3 good rains | NAERLS |
| Rice (rain-fed) | Guinea savanna | First two weeks of June | IITA |
| Rice (rain-fed) | Sudan savanna | Third to fourth week of June | IITA |
| Rice (rain-fed) | Savanna in general | Mid-May to mid-June | NAERLS |
| Tomato | North | Dry season, with irrigation (variety Roma VF) | NIHORT |
| Tomato | Any zone, rainy-season sowing | Stake the plants at the peak of the rains | NIHORT |

The rice windows differ a little between sources. A safe approach is to plant when the rains are established, from mid-May to June in the savanna and earlier in the forest zone.

## Off-season rice

FAO lists two rice seasons for both north and south:

| Season | South: plant | South: harvest | North: plant | North: harvest |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Main season | April-May | August-October | June-July | November-December |
| Off season | November-December | March-April | January-February | May-June |

The off-season crop needs irrigation or a water source such as fadama, and in the north it can be hurt by cool nights.

## Tips

- Plant right after a good rain, not on the first shower.
- For crops that need fertiliser at a set time, such as maize, count the weeks from the day you plant.
- Check the zones document to see which zone your state is in.

# Which Zone Each Nigerian State Belongs To

Zone borders do not follow state borders, so some states cover more than one zone. The table gives the main zone for each of the 36 states and the Federal Capital Territory (FCT), following the National Agricultural Extension and Research Liaison Services (NAERLS) list. If you farm near the edge of a zone, expect conditions in between.

| Zone | States |
|---|---|
| Sahel savanna | Yobe |
| Sudan savanna | Borno, Jigawa, Kano, Katsina, Zamfara, Sokoto, Kebbi |
| Northern Guinea savanna | Kaduna, Bauchi, Gombe |
| Southern Guinea savanna | Niger, Adamawa |
| Derived savanna | Nasarawa, Kwara, FCT, Plateau, Kogi, Benue, Taraba |
| Humid forest | Oyo, Osun, Ogun, Ekiti, Enugu, Lagos, Ondo, Edo, Delta, Bayelsa, Rivers, Anambra, Imo, Ebonyi, Abia, Akwa Ibom, Cross River |

## Things to know

- Parts of some states belong to other zones. For example, Borno, Sokoto and Kebbi have some local government areas in a different zone, and high parts of Plateau State around Jos belong to the cooler mid-altitude zone.
- Other sources draw the zone lines differently. Some place states such as Kano, Kaduna and Bauchi partly in the Guinea savanna, and some place Kwara, Kogi and Benue partly in the Guinea savannas and not only in the derived savanna.
- The coastal states of the Niger Delta also contain mangrove and freshwater swamp areas, which are very wet and often waterlogged.
- Use the zone as a starting point. Local rainfall, soil and altitude matter too, so ask your local extension officer when in doubt.

# Growing Cashew in Nigeria

The guidance comes from CRIN, the institute responsible for cashew research in Nigeria. The source is a 2010 conference presentation, so pesticide advice from it is left out where the products are old, and its yield figures are inconsistent, so confirm them with CRIN.

## Soil and site

- Cashew needs soil at least 100-150 cm deep. Avoid very clayey or very sandy soils.
- The source gives no rainfall or temperature requirements.

## Land preparation and planting

- Clear the bush in the dry season without bulldozing, and avoid burning the whole plot. Uproot trees that host cashew pests.
- Plough and harrow with the first rains and dig planting holes 45 x 45 x 45 cm.
- Plant or transplant after heavy rains and backfill with topsoil mixed with organic matter. For direct sowing, put two nuts per point with the stalk end facing up.
- Choose seed from good trees, harvested in the current season, free of disease and cleaned of apple remnants. Nuts weighing 6-13 g are preferred.
- CRIN has released improved genotypes and distributes seed and seedlings. Clonal material is propagated by marcotting (air layering).

## Spacing

| Tree type | Spacing | Trees per hectare |
|---|---|---|
| Dwarf or savannah types | 4.5 x 4.5 m | 494 |
| Dwarf or savannah types | 6 x 6 m | 278 |
| Dwarf or savannah types | 7.5 x 7.5 m | 178 |
| Dwarf or savannah types | 9 x 9 m | 123 |
| Large open-pollinated trees | 10 x 10 m | 100 |
| Large open-pollinated trees | 12 x 12 m | 69 |

Triangular layouts fit more trees, while rectangular layouts leave room for more years of intercropping.

## Intercropping and weeding

- Intercrop for the first four years with low, non-climbing legumes and food crops that do not host cashew pests, such as maize, cassava and cocoyam. Avoid cotton, which hosts the Helopeltis bug.
- Weed 3-5 times a year. Mulch in the first dry season after planting.

## Pruning and fire protection

- Prune at 2-3 years to form a single stem, lightly prune after each harvest, and remove dead or diseased branches every year.
- Paint large cut surfaces with 1% Bordeaux mixture.
- Clear a 15-20 m fire break around the plot.

## Fertiliser

The source says low soil fertility limits yield but gives no fertiliser type or rate. Ask CRIN or an extension officer.

## Pests and diseases

- Stem girdlers: remove infested branches and hand-pick and crush the adults. Protect weaver ants and spiders, which prey on mirids, by not spraying their nests.
- Root and stem borers: keep the farm clean.
- Red-banded thrips and leaf rollers: the source lists old insecticides, so ask an extension officer which products are approved today.
- Dieback, floral blight and shoot anthracnose: remove and destroy affected twigs, grow resistant varieties and ask for approved fungicides.

## Harvest and drying

- Pick only fully mature fruits that have fallen naturally, and separate the nuts from the apples straight away. Do not heap nuts or leave them in bags for long.
- Dry nuts on a concrete slab for 2-3 days until they rattle, reaching about 8-9% moisture.
- Processors want dry, sound nuts of uniform size, not smaller than 5.6 g, with a kernel content of about 26%. Pack in jute bags and stack on pallets in a ventilated, leak-proof store.
- Improved CRIN clones give nuts of 3-8 g and about 12-15 kg per tree per year. The source's per-hectare yield figures are inconsistent, so they are not repeated here.

# Growing Cassava in Nigeria

Cassava is one of Nigeria's most important food crops. It is propagated from stem cuttings (stakes), not from seed, and its roots are processed into garri, fufu, starch and other products.

## Soil and land preparation

- Cassava does best on deep, well-drained loamy soil. Avoid very sandy or very clayey soils.
- On deep, well-drained sandy loam, plant on flat land. On shallow or clayey soils, till and make ridges.
- Where water tends to stand, plant on ridges or mounds. This is common practice in the rainforest and derived savanna zones.

## When to plant

Plant when the rains are steady.

| Zone | Planting time |
|---|---|
| Rainforest | March to November |
| Derived savanna | April to August |
| Southern Guinea savanna | May to July |
| Northern Guinea savanna | July to August |

## Planting material

- Take stakes from healthy plants that are 8 to 18 months old. Use the hardwood and semi-hardwood parts of the stem. Avoid the soft shoot tips, which die easily.
- A standard stake is 20-25 cm long with at least 5 nodes (buds). Cut with a sharp tool and do not bruise the stem.
- Plant soon after cutting. If you must store stakes, keep them for 3-5 days in transparent polythene bags, which also lets them begin to sprout before planting.
- About 50 bundles of stems (about 1 m long each) are needed for one hectare.
- Improved varieties named by IITA include TMS 30572, 4(2)1425, 92/0326 and NR 8082. Try a variety on a small area of your own farm first.

## How to plant

- Vertical (buds up, two-thirds of the stake buried): suits sandy soils, but harvest is harder.
- Horizontal (stake buried 3-5 cm deep): gives many stems and roots and suits loamy, rich soils.
- Inclined (buds up, two-thirds buried at about 45 degrees): suits shallow and clayey soils and makes harvest easier.

## Spacing

- 1 m by 1 m gives 10,000 plants per hectare, the best population for high root yield.
- IITA also gives 0.9 m by 0.9 m, about 12,300 plants per hectare, as a starting population.
- Spacing changes when cassava is intercropped. Varieties with few branches suit intercropping best.

## Weed control

- Weed at least 3 times by hand with a hoe or cutlass. Forest areas need more weeding than savanna.
- Before planting, harrowing the land two or three times also helps reduce weeds.
- Herbicides are an option. Always follow the label and ask an extension officer, and do not spray just before or after heavy rain.

## Fertiliser

- A soil test is best. Without one, use the field history and the plants growing there as a guide. Land covered with Chromolaena odorata may need no fertiliser, while land with spear grass or sparse vegetation needs it.
- Forest zone, continuous cultivation: 200 kg per hectare of NPK 15:15:15 at 4-6 weeks after planting, then 100 kg per hectare of muriate of potash at 14-16 weeks.
- Savanna zone: 200 kg per hectare of NPK 15:15:15 at 4-6 weeks, then 50 kg per hectare of muriate of potash.
- Put the fertiliser in small holes about 5 cm deep and 10 cm from the plant, and do not apply to dry soil.

## Pests and diseases

- Cassava mosaic disease is spread by whiteflies. Plant resistant varieties, remove infected plants early, and intercrop with tall plants such as maize.
- Cassava bacterial blight: plant tolerant varieties, and cut and burn infected plants.
- Green spider mite: use resistant varieties.
- Termites: let stakes sprout first for 3-5 days, plant in moist soil, clear dead wood, and destroy anthills.
- Grasshoppers are most damaging in the dry season from September.
- Grass cutters and bush fowls: use traps or fencing and keep the farm weed-free.

See the Pest Guide entries for cassava mealybug, cassava mosaic disease and cassava bacterial blight.

## Harvest

- Roots are ready 9 to 18 months after planting. Yields of 15 to over 50 tonnes per hectare are possible.
- Harvest only when you have a buyer or are ready to process. Avoid bruising the roots and process them within 48 hours.
- Dry matter content, the main quality factor, is usually highest after the rains and during harmattan.
- Young leaves can also be harvested as a vegetable, and stems can be sold as planting material.

# Growing Cocoa in Nigeria

**Source note.** No official CRIN or NAERLS cocoa manual could be opened. This document rests on a newspaper article and an unattributed course text, so treat the figures as a starting point and confirm them with CRIN or a local extension officer.

## Where it grows

Cocoa grows well in rainforest areas. The article says over 14 states are suitable, including Taraba and Zamfara, but most Nigerian cocoa comes from the south-west and south-south (see the zone documents for the humid forest zone).

## Site and shade

- Cocoa does not tolerate scorching sun or heavy shade. Young seedlings need shade.
- On land already under annual crops, farmers intercrop cassava or plantain with young cocoa for shade. On virgin land, leave some standing forest trees as canopy (smaller trees about 4 m apart).
- The course text names plantain as a good nurse shade and says banana should be avoided, because it competes with cocoa for moisture in the dry season.
- Cocoa lives for decades, so think about land tenure before planting. One farmer in the article preferred buying land to leasing it, since leases may not be renewed.

## Planting material

- Eight improved varieties developed at CRIN are available in Nigeria. They mature early, resist some pests and out-yield old varieties.
- The most reliable source is CRIN in Ibadan, which supplies the TC1 to TC8 series and budded material. The Cocoa Association of Nigeria says it raises 3-5 million seedlings a year and gives them free to farmers in Ondo, Cross River, Ogun, Ekiti and Osun.

## Nursery and planting

- Start the nursery early in the season so seedlings are ready for the rains. The article put the nursery window at roughly November to December, with transplanting the following rainy season.
- Space plants 3 x 3 m (10 x 10 ft), which gives about 1,111 plants per hectare. The article also cites 1,000-1,200 trees per hectare. To end with 1,200 fruiting trees, plant at least 1,500 seedlings, because some die.
- Polybag seedlings need about 7 days to rest on site, and should be planted as soon as they arrive.

## Weeding and mulching

- Course text: weed in rows three times a year and slash the avenues 4-6 times a year.
- Mulch about 15 cm deep towards the end of the rains, leaving a small clear ring around each seedling to reduce termite damage.

## Fertiliser

The course text gives 100 kg N, 20 kg P and 70 kg K per hectare per year for cocoa, split into instalments at the start of main leaf growth, main flowering and main fruit growth. This is an unattributed figure and was not tested in Nigeria in the source, so confirm it with CRIN or a soil test.

## Yield timeline (improved varieties, per the article)

- Fruiting starts at about 18 months.
- First harvest is around 300 kg per hectare at about 30 months.
- At 5-6 years, about 1 tonne per hectare, and above 1.5 tonnes per hectare from about year 8.

## Pests and diseases

- Black pod (a fungus-like disease): remove infected pods, weed to let air in, and use copper-based sprays such as lime Bordeaux mixture. See the black pod guide for more.
- Swollen shoot virus, spread by mealybugs: cut out infected trees.
- Mirids (capsids) and pod-husk borers are controlled with insecticides, so ask an extension officer which are approved today. The course text names an old termite chemical that is no longer advisable, so that is left out.

## Harvest, drying and storage

- A pod takes about 150-180 days from pollination to ripening. Harvest about twice a month. The main seasons are about September to March and April to August.
- Dry beans to about 7% moisture (6-8%). Store them off the ground and away from walls, and keep them away from maize, tobacco and other foodstuffs.
- Grading: the text allows under 3% each of slaty, mouldy and other defective beans for Grade I. Grade II allows under 5% slaty, 4% mouldy and 5% other defectives.
- The sources do not describe fermentation. Ask CRIN or a local buying agent for fermentation advice, since it strongly affects bean quality.

# Growing Cotton in Nigeria

**Source note.** No official Nigerian extension guide for cotton was found. This document combines research trials in the Guinea savanna, so use it as a guide and check local details (varieties, planting dates, inputs) with an extension officer or cotton buyer.

## Where and when

- The trials were in the northern and southern Guinea savanna (Samaru, Mokwa and Kabba).
- Sowing on 1 June was used in the Kabba trials. The Samaru and Mokwa trials studied late sowing, in late July, and found nitrogen still paid off there.
- Cotton is a rain-fed crop in these trials. Sow once the rains are established.

## Variety

- Newer hybrid varieties out-yielded the local seed by about 58% in the Kabba trial.
- In the older Samaru and Mokwa trials, the variety Super Okra gave the best yield and matured early. Improved seed from a research institute or a cotton buyer is a better bet than farm-saved seed.

## Spacing and plant population

- After germination, thin to one plant per stand. In the Kabba trial, plants were thinned three weeks after sowing to 20 cm apart within the row.
- In the older trials, a 75 cm row spacing did better than the then-recommended 90 cm at Mokwa. At Samaru, higher plant populations gave higher yields for late sowing, but at Mokwa the trend reversed, so the best population depends on the site.

## Fertiliser

- In the Kabba trial, NPK 20:10:10 plus boron at 80 kg per hectare gave the highest yield. Nitrogen was split into two equal parts, one after sowing and one at first flowering.
- In the older trials, nitrogen raised seed cotton yield clearly. The best economic rate was about 64 kg N per hectare, and farmers working by hoe could profit from about 40 kg N per hectare.

## Weeds

Weed by hand about every three weeks in the early season (the Kabba trial removed weeds at three-week intervals).

## Pests

Bollworms, thrips and aphids are the main insect problems named in the Kabba trial, and were sprayed at peak flowering and boll setting. Product choice and rates change over time and some older products are restricted, so follow the label and ask an extension officer which products are approved today.

## Harvest

Pick bolls when they are open and dry. The research trials used chemical defoliation before harvest, which is a commercial practice, so ask a buyer or extension officer before using it.

# Growing Cowpea in Nigeria

Cowpea (beans) is Nigeria's most important food legume and is grown mainly in the northern savannas. It fixes nitrogen, so it improves the soil for the cereal that follows, and the haulms (stalks) make useful dry-season fodder. National yields rose from about 409 kg per hectare in 1980-84 to about 900 kg per hectare in 2014-18.

## Site and land preparation

- Use well-drained sandy loam and avoid waterlogged soils.
- Clear shrubs and stubble. Till deep enough to break any hardpan that would block the taproot.

## When to plant

Plant when the rains are established. Photoperiod-sensitive varieties flower late if sown too early, so they go in later.

| Zone | Early and medium varieties (determinate) | Medium and late varieties (indeterminate) |
|---|---|---|
| Sahel | 14-28 June | 20-25 June |
| Sudan savanna | 25 June - 24 July | 19-22 July |
| Northern Guinea savanna | 25 July - 8 August | 28 July - 3 August |
| Southern Guinea savanna | 25-30 August | 24-28 August |

## Varieties and seed

- Choose by maturity, yield, drought and Striga tolerance, and pest and disease resistance. Buy certified seed from a recognised source, not the open market.
- SAMPEA 18 and SAMPEA 19 are medium-maturity varieties (70-75 days) that tolerate Striga and Alectra, yield about 2.3-2.4 t per hectare, and suit the Sudan and northern Guinea savannas.
- Use sound seed without holes or wrinkles. Dress it with a seed dressing before sowing.

## Seed rate and spacing

| Type | Spacing | Seed rate |
|---|---|---|
| Erect, early (sole crop) | 50 x 20 cm | 30 kg per hectare |
| Semi-erect, medium or late (sole crop) | 75 x 30 cm | 20 kg per hectare |
| Semi-erect, intercropped with a cereal | 75 x 25 cm | 25 kg per hectare |
| Spreading (prostrate) | 75 x 50 cm | 15 kg per hectare |

Sow 3 seeds per hill, 2.5-5 cm deep, and thin to 2 plants two weeks after planting. For intercropping, plant cowpea 5-7 weeks after the cereal.

## Fertiliser

- Phosphorus: about 30 kg P2O5 per hectare (about 4 bags of single superphosphate) at land preparation or just before sowing.
- Nitrogen: about 15 kg N per hectare (2 bags of NPK 15:15:15) at planting, only where soil nitrogen is poor. Too much nitrogen gives leaves and little grain.

## Weed control

The critical period is 5-8 weeks after planting. Hoe twice, at about 2 and 4-5 weeks, with a third weeding before flowering if needed (especially where Imperata grass grows).

## Insect pests

Plan 2-3 sprays, timed to the crop stage and not to the calendar: the first about 30-35 days after planting at bud formation (for thrips and early Maruca), the second about 10 days later at full flowering and podding, and a third if needed. Spray in the early morning or late evening, avoid windy conditions, and use only products approved for cowpea at the label rate.

| Pest | Damage | Reported loss |
|---|---|---|
| Flower thrips | Damage flowers and buds | 20-100% |
| Maruca pod borer | Bores into buds, flowers and pods | 20-80% |
| Pod-sucking bugs | Shrivelled pods | 30-70% |
| Aphids and whiteflies | Suck sap and spread viruses | Variable |

See the Pest Guide entry for legume pod borer (Maruca).

## Diseases

Fusarium wilt, Cercospora leaf spot, bacterial blight, scab and viruses are the main diseases. Resistant varieties are the main control, and seed-borne diseases are reduced by clean seed. Remove virus-infected plants.

## Striga and Alectra

Striga seed can stay alive in soil for over 20 years. Combine rotation with cereals, nitrogen fertiliser and trap crops, and plant resistant varieties, which are the most effective and cheapest option.

## Harvest and storage

- Harvest as soon as pods are mature and dry, by hand-picking. Erect early varieties may need one picking, and spreading types two or three.
- Sun-dry pods on a tarpaulin or platform, thresh, winnow and clean the seed.
- Store only well-dried seed at 7-8% moisture (it should crack when bitten), in hermetic containers such as PICS triple bags. Clean the store first and keep rodents out.

# Growing Groundnut in Nigeria

Groundnut is an important food and cash crop in the northern savannas. The guidance below comes from a 2020 handbook written for north-east Nigeria (Borno and Adamawa), so varieties and dates fit that area best.

## When to plant

Sow once the rains are well established and the soil is steadily moist. In the Sudan savanna that is usually near the end of June, and earlier in the Guinea savannas. A dry-season crop with irrigation can be planted from 15 January to 10 February, or in mid-October at the end of the rains to avoid harmattan cold.

## Varieties

- Samnut 24: 80-90 days, resists rosette, does well in the dry season.
- Samnut 27: 80-85 days, resists rosette and tolerates leaf spots, about 3.5 t of pods per hectare.
- Samnut 23: 90-100 days, resists leaf spot.
- Samnut 28: 100-105 days, resists rosette, about 3.0 t per hectare.
- Use clean, disease-free, unbroken, mature seed.

## Spacing

- Bunch types: 75 x 20 cm, 75 x 10 cm or 50 x 20 cm.
- Spreading and semi-spreading types: 75 x 20 cm or 75 x 25 cm.
- Sow 2 seeds per hole, 5 cm deep. Thinning is normally not needed. The handbook gives no fixed seed rate because it depends on the variety, seed size and spacing.

## Fertiliser and gypsum

- A soil test is best. Lime only if soil pH is below 5.8.
- Without a soil test, broadcast 1 bag of NPK 15:15:15 and 2 bags of single superphosphate per hectare at land preparation. Organic manure at 3-5 t per hectare also helps.
- Gypsum: 400 kg per hectare at peak flowering and pegging, which supplies the calcium the pods need.

## Weed control

Weeds can cut yield by about 30%, and up to 60% under poor management. Weed 2-3 times, once before flowering and once during or after pegging. Once pegging starts, avoid disturbing the soil around the plants.

## Pests and diseases

- Rosette virus, spread by aphids: plant resistant varieties and sow early. Intercropping with cereals helps.
- Early and late leaf spot: use resistant varieties, sow early, and rotate with cereals.
- Rust: act as soon as it appears, and destroy volunteer plants and crop debris.
- Aphids can cause up to 40% yield loss. Destroy volunteer plants and weeds.
- Leaf miner, thrips and termites are also important, and rotation with cereals helps.
- If a spray is needed, use only approved products at the label rate and ask an extension officer.

See the Pest Guide entry for groundnut rosette.

## Harvest and drying

- Harvest when 70-80% of pods are mature. Signs are a darkened inner shell and a thin, tight seed coat.
- Do not delay. Mature pods can sprout and pegs weaken, so pods are left in the soil. Harvesting too early lowers yield and seed quality.
- Shake off the soil and dry the plants inverted, pods facing up, for 2-3 days. Then dry pods on clean sheets or raised platforms, never on bare soil, to 8-10% moisture.

## Aflatoxin and storage

Pods bagged wet for even 24 hours can rot and develop aflatoxin, a poison. To prevent it:
- avoid late harvest, damaged pods and drying on the soil
- remove mouldy, discoloured and shrivelled kernels
- do not shell by beating or trampling

Store kernels below 10% moisture in clean bags off the floor, in a dry, ventilated place, and do not mix new stock with old. Against groundnut bruchid, store kernels with about 500 g of dried neem leaves per 10 kg in a sealed container.

## Dry-season crop

A dry-season crop needs a dependable water source. Irrigate every 7-10 days by furrow or sprinkler, use aphid- and rosette-resistant varieties, and mulch with black polythene if planting in December or January.

# Growing Maize in Nigeria

Maize is grown across most of Nigeria. The detailed guidance below comes from IITA's guide for northern Nigeria (the Guinea and Sudan savannas). In the wetter south there are two rainy seasons, so a second maize crop in the year is often possible.

## When to plant

Plant as soon as the rains are established, ideally right after a good rain.

| Zone | Planting time |
|---|---|
| Southern Guinea savanna | Mid-May to second week of June |
| Northern Guinea savanna | Last week of June to first week of July |
| Sudan savanna | First to second week of July |

## Soil and land preparation

- Many soils are low in nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium and also in zinc and copper. Without fertiliser, yields can be under 1 tonne per hectare.
- Sow on ridges 75 cm apart. Zero tillage does not reduce yield.

## Seed

- Use certified seed.
- Early-maturing varieties suit the Sudan savanna, where the rainy season is short. IITA names several open-pollinated SAMMAZ varieties for each zone (for example SAMMAZ 15, 27, 40, 48 and 51) and hybrids such as Oba Super 6.
- Dress the seed with a recommended seed dressing before planting, which controls most seed- and soil-borne diseases. Wash your hands afterwards.

## Spacing and seed rate

- Space plants 75 cm between rows and 50 cm within rows, with 2 plants per stand. That is about 53,000 plants per hectare.
- Seed rate is 15-20 kg per hectare. Sow 3 seeds per hole and thin to 2 plants 1-2 weeks after the seeds sprout.

## Fertiliser

Maize fertiliser is applied in two doses.

1. First dose at planting or within the first week: about 6 bags of NPK 15:15:15. Delaying beyond week 1 sharply reduces the yield of early and extra-early varieties.
2. Second dose, about 2 bags of urea: at 4-5 weeks after planting in the Guinea savannas, and at 3-4 weeks for extra-early varieties in the Sudan savanna. Do the second weeding before this dose, and do not delay it beyond 4 weeks.

Place fertiliser 5-8 cm from the plant and cover it with soil. A field guide is about 2 bottle caps of NPK per stand at week 1 and 1 cap of urea at week 4.

A separate guideline states the nutrient totals per hectare as 100 kg nitrogen, 50 kg single superphosphate and 50 kg muriate of potash. This is a different way of stating the rate and is not a bag count.

## Weed control

- By hand, weed at 2 weeks and again at 4-5 weeks after planting. Fields with Imperata grass need more weeding.
- Herbicides named by IITA are glyphosate before land preparation and atrazine, Primextra Gold or Dual Gold at planting or the day after. Do not use atrazine where you intercrop with legumes. Follow the label.
- Striga (witchweed): rotate with soybean, plant tolerant varieties and use the recommended fertiliser.

## Pests and diseases

- Fall armyworm: IITA advises controlling it within the first 3 weeks after planting. Scout the field often and ask an extension officer about approved products.
- Stem borers and grasshoppers occur from time to time and are rarely serious.

See the Pest Guide entries for fall armyworm, stem borers and maize streak virus.

## Harvest and yield

- Fresh (green) maize: harvest when the silk turns brown, about 50-70 days after planting.
- Dry grain: harvest when the grain is dry, about 80-110 days after planting, depending on variety.
- With recommended practices, expect about 2,500-3,500 kg of grain per hectare.

## Storage

- Dry grain to 10-12% moisture and keep it below 12% to prevent mould.
- Clean the store thoroughly before filling it.
- For storage beyond 6 months, use airtight hermetic bags such as PICS bags, which need no chemicals. Fumigation with phosphine tablets is another option, but the gas is toxic, so follow the label and use trained help.

# Growing Mango and Citrus in Nigeria

## Citrus

The citrus guidance comes from the National Horticultural Research Institute (NIHORT).

**Climate and site.** Citrus can be grown from the coast to northern Nigeria. Choose flat, accessible land near a permanent water source, with fertile, well-drained soil that has no hardpan or rock. Sandy loam is best. Avoid salty soils.

**Rootstock and planting material.** Rootstock depends on the zone: Cleopatra mandarin for rainforest areas and rough lemon for savanna areas. Plant only budded seedlings, because unbudded seedlings fruit late and may not be true to type. Buy from reliable sources such as NIHORT, state ministries of agriculture or ADPs.

**Nursery and budding.** Raise rootstock seedlings from fresh seed, and bud them when the stem is 0.6-0.7 cm thick, using budwood from a fruiting tree of the variety you want. After the bud takes, cut the rootstock 5-10 cm above the bud and remove rootstock shoots every 3-4 weeks.

**When to plant.** Southern Nigeria: May. Northern Nigeria: July or August. Plant when the rains are steady, and dig holes in advance. Set each seedling at the same depth it grew in the nursery, and firm the soil.

**Fertiliser.** Young trees usually need none in year 1. From 2 to 4 years, give each tree 500 g of NPK 15:15:15 twice a year. From 5 to 10 years, give 2 kg of NPK 15:15:15 plus 600 g of potassium oxide per tree, twice a year (June and September).

**Water.** Dry-season watering is critical (November to February): 10 litres at the base of each tree twice a week.

**Weeds.** In orchards over 7 years old, slash at least three times a year, with the last cut at the start of the dry season, and ring-weed around each tree.

**Intercropping.** Groundnut, maize, cowpea, soybean, okra, pepper and leafy vegetables can be grown between young trees until the canopy closes. Do not intercrop cassava, plantain or pineapple with citrus. Keep about 1 m between trees and the other crops.

**Pests and diseases.** Fruit flies, fruit-piercing moths and bugs damage fruit. Harvest at about 50% ripeness and bury fallen fruit deeply to reduce losses. Viral diseases spread through budding tools and budwood, so sterilise tools and use disease-free budwood. NIHORT's guide names several chemical products, so ask an extension officer which products are approved today.

**Harvest and yield.** Yields from year 7 are 20-40 t per hectare, depending on variety.

## Mango

No Nigerian growing guide for mango was found, so this part is short. Ask NIHORT or a local extension officer for local spacing, varieties and planting dates.

**Where it grows.** NIHORT says mango can be grown in all of Nigeria's agro-ecological zones. About 120,000-130,000 hectares are planted, and Benue has many orchards. Nigeria produced about 968,780 tonnes in 2021. Mango is hardy and drought-tolerant, and needs little water once established.

**General guidance (from a University of Florida guide).** The following is general advice from Florida and may differ from Nigerian conditions.
- Mango needs well-drained soil, and long flooding kills trees.
- A dry period before and during flowering is best for fruit set.
- Most commercial mango is propagated by grafting or budding, and grafted trees give marketable fruit within three to five years.
- Anthracnose and powdery mildew are the main diseases, and mites, scale insects and thrips are the main pests.
- Fruit is picked mature but still green, when the shoulders fill out and the flesh near the seed turns from white to yellow.

**Challenges in Nigeria.** Fruit flies limit exports, and large amounts of fruit are wasted at peak harvest because of poor storage and processing. NIHORT has developed juice, puree, flakes and jam from mango to reduce waste.

# Growing Oil Palm in Nigeria

**Source note.** No NIFOR (Nigerian Institute for Oil Palm Research) guide could be opened. This document uses a long research trial near Benin City and a short course text, so it is thin on fertiliser and planting-material advice. Ask NIFOR or a local extension officer for those.

## Climate and soil

- The course text says oil palm needs rainfall of at least 1,500 mm, temperatures of about 27-35°C and deep, well-drained soil.
- The trial site near Benin City had around 2,000 mm of rain a year in two seasons, on sandy-surfaced ferralitic soil.

## Planting density

- A 16-year trial at Obaretin, near Benin City, tested equilateral triangle planting at 7.5 to 9.5 m between palms. The best range was 143-160 palms per hectare (about 8.5-9 m apart). The authors suggest about 151 palms per hectare (about 8.75 m) as a good compromise.
- Thinning the plots from 180 to 154 palms per hectare at year 8 did not pay off. Cumulative yield per hectare was 95% of the unthinned plot at year 8 and 97% at year 16.
- This was one estate trial with one planting material (Pobè C1001F, a Deli x La Mé type), so smallholder results may differ.

## Fertiliser

The trial gave a uniform rate per palm for the first four years, then a uniform rate per hectare, and adjusted it each year using leaf analysis so that nutrients never limited yield. The rates themselves were not given in the text available. Without leaf analysis, ask an extension officer for local rates.

## Harvest

The trial harvested every 10 days. Keeping the circle around each palm weeded makes loose fruits easy to find and reduces losses, but it is a major cost.

## Problems seen in the trial

- Young palms lost to termites and grass cutters (cane rats), and occasional rhinoceros-beetle (Oryctes) attacks.
- Ganoderma (a stem rot): about 2 deaths per 1,000 palms over 16 years.
- Fusarium wilt: no deaths, because the material used was tolerant.
- Wind toppling and lightning strikes.
- At very high densities the trunks stretched (etiolation).

# Growing Pepper, Okra and Onion in Nigeria

## Pepper

**Climate.** Pepper grows best at 21-27°C. Growth slows below about 15°C, flowers drop in excess heat above 32°C, and few fruits set above 35°C. Early varieties need at least 3 months of warm weather, and most others 4-5 months.

**Nursery.** Make seedbeds 120-150 cm wide. Mix rotted manure or NPK 15:15:15 (22 g per square metre) into the bed. Sow thinly in rows 10-12 cm apart, mulch with dry grass until seedlings emerge, and water daily. Raise the beds about 15 cm in the rainy season. Cut watering back for three days before transplanting to harden the seedlings.

**Transplanting.** Transplant uniform six-week-old seedlings, in the early morning or late evening, and fill gaps about a week later. In northern Nigeria planting can continue to the end of the rains, around September, using leftover soil moisture and some irrigation.

**Spacing.** Ridges 75-90 cm wide with plants 45-60 cm apart. Ridges 75 cm wide with 45 cm between plants give about 29,600 plants per hectare.

**Fertiliser.** On loam and heavier soils, mix in 250-500 kg per hectare of NPK 15:15:15 about a week before transplanting, and top-dress with about 65 kg N per hectare when several fruits have set. Light sandy soils need more (750-1,000 kg per hectare). Add 25-37.5 t per hectare of manure or compost each year, and lime very acid soils.

**Water.** The crop needs about 600-800 mm per season, roughly 15-20 irrigations by basin or furrow.

**Weeds.** Hand-weed shallowly three times at three-week intervals, starting three weeks after transplanting.

**Pests and diseases.** Aphids, cutworms, flea beetles, borers and hornworms attack pepper, and hornworms can be handpicked in small fields. Do not plant pepper where pepper or tomato grew the year before, to reduce bacterial spot and blight. Treat seed and seedbeds to prevent damping-off, and grow on well-drained, fertile soil to limit blossom-end rot and sunscald. The 2016 bulletin lists some old pesticides that are now banned or restricted, so ask an extension officer which products are approved today.

**Harvest and yield.** Harvest green-mature fruit when it is about 10 cm long, dark green and firm. Pick by hand with the stem attached. Farmers' yields are around 9 t per hectare, and up to 15 t per hectare is possible with recommended practices.

## Okra

The only source found for okra is a general Nigerian farming website, so treat this part as a starting point and confirm details with a local extension officer.

- **Climate and soil.** Okra grows best at about 25-30°C on fertile, well-drained, deep loam.
- **Planting.** Sow seed directly, or raise seedlings in a nursery. Soaking seed overnight in tepid water speeds germination.
- **Spacing.** About 30-60 cm between plants and 90-120 cm between rows.
- **Fertility and water.** Add farmyard manure or compost. Water during the dry season.
- **Pests and diseases.** Stink bugs, aphids, corn earworms and Fusarium wilt.
- **Harvest.** Pick every other day while pods are 5-7.5 cm long, cutting the stem just above the cap. Wear gloves for spiny varieties. Pods keep for 1-2 days in a cool place, and slices can be sun-dried.

## Onion

Onion in Nigeria is mainly a dry-season, irrigated crop.

**Climate and soil.** Onion grows best at 15-25°C on well-drained, medium-textured soil with a pH of 6-7. Warm weather slows bulb formation, and clear, dry days suit the crop.

**Season.** Sow in seedbeds and plant out between October and January.

**Nursery.** Sow in rows 10-15 cm apart on a seedbed 120 cm wide, in grooves about 12 mm deep. Mix the seed with fine sand, cover lightly and mulch, then water once or twice a week.

**Transplanting.** Transplant at 6-8 weeks, with roots spread naturally and soil firmed around each plant. The bulletin gives no spacing.

**Fertiliser.** A general rate is 65 kg N, 40 kg P and 45 kg K per hectare, applied four weeks after transplanting. Use a soil test where possible.

**Water.** Irrigate every 5-7 days in the field, about 15-20 times per season (350-550 mm in total). Onion is sensitive to water stress, and waterlogging damages it badly.

**Weeds.** Weed early, since onion competes poorly, and take care not to damage seedlings.

**Pests and diseases.** Thrips, maggots, cutworms and leaf miner are the main insects. Onion twister, purple blotch, downy mildew and yellow dwarf virus are the main diseases. Use field sanitation, early planting, well-drained land and resistant varieties. The 2016 bulletin names old pesticides, many now banned, so ask an extension officer for approved products.

**Harvest, curing and storage.** Harvest when the necks soften and the tops fall over, in dry weather and without bruising. Lay bulbs on their sides to ripen for several days, then cure by drying the neck. Smallholders can store bulbs on bamboo or wooden stacks with good airflow for up to 6 months. Good management gives 10-12 t per hectare.

# Growing Pineapple in Nigeria

The guidance below comes from the National Horticultural Research Institute (NIHORT). The guide does not cover climate, planting time, flower induction or pests and diseases, so ask NIHORT or a local extension officer about those.

## Varieties

NIHORT names Smooth Cayenne, Sugarloaf and MD 2.

## Soil and site

- Use well-drained soil rich in organic matter, preferably acidic (pH 4.5-5.5) and low in salts.
- Avoid heavy or waterlogged soils. Land that was under Chromolaena odorata is better than land under grass.

## Planting material

| Material | Where it comes from | Time to fruit |
|---|---|---|
| Ratoon suckers | Ground level | 12-14 months |
| Slips (preferred) | Base of the fruit | 14-16 months |
| Suckers | Side shoots on the stem | 18-20 months |
| Crowns | Top of the fruit | Up to 24 months |

Sort planting material by size, type and condition before planting.

## Nursery for crowns

- Split a 100 g crown into four equal parts, dip the pieces in a fungicide solution to prevent rot, and air-dry overnight.
- Plant in 90 cm wide beds of topsoil and weathered sawdust (1:1). Sprouts appear after about six weeks, and are then moved to 1 m beds, 15 cm apart.
- Water the beds twice a week in the dry season, and add manure or urea three weeks after planting to speed growth.

## Spacing

Mark the field from a baseline with lines crossing at right angles, and plant in double rows at 50 x 50 x 100 cm.

## Fertiliser

Apply 300-350 kg per hectare of NPK 20:10:10 or 15:15:15 in three split doses during the rainy season, adjusted to the soil's nutrient status.

## Weed control

Weed by hand with a cutlass or hoe. Mulching with grass reduces weeding and nutrient loss, and NIHORT has developed plastic mulch. Herbicides such as diuron and ametryne are also named, so ask an extension officer about rates.

## Harvest and yield

- Harvest takes 15-18 months from planting, and begins 4-5 months after flower induction.
- Fully ripe fruit suits local markets, and mature green fruit can be exported.
- NIHORT reports an average yield of 80 t per hectare with good management, and 90-100 t per hectare is achievable.

# Growing Plantain and Banana in Nigeria

The guidance below comes from IITA's reference manual on plantain for West Africa. Bananas are grown in much the same way, but the manual is about plantain, so check banana-specific details with an extension officer.

## Climate and site

- Plantain wants an average temperature of about 30°C and at least 100 mm of well-spread rain every month, with a short dry season. Irrigation is generally not practical for smallholders.
- Choose well-drained, gently sloping land that does not flood, with the water table deeper than 50 cm and soil rich in organic matter.

## Planting material

- The best conventional material is the sword sucker. Peepers, maiden suckers and corm bits also work, and plants from tissue culture are a promising option.
- Collect suckers from an old, declining field or a multiplication plot. Peel the corm, cut the pseudostem a few centimetres above it, and dry the sucker for a few days out of the sun.
- Plant within a week. Storing suckers more than two weeks lowers later yields.

## Spacing and planting

- Use 3 x 2 m (about 1,670 plants per hectare) or 2.5 x 2.5 m (1,600 plants per hectare).
- Dig holes at least 30 x 30 x 30 cm and keep the topsoil and bottom soil apart.
- Plant in the middle of the rainy season for off-season fruit and better prices. Avoid the last months of the rains.

## Mulching and weeds

- Mulch is the best weed control. Cover the soil completely, using elephant grass or other plant material.
- Weed about every 6-8 weeks at first. Weeding becomes less important once the canopy closes, about 5-6 months after planting.

## Fertiliser

- Start one month after planting, or with the first rains in an existing field. Spread it in a ring about 50 cm across and do not dig it in. Apply none in the dry season.
- Potassium and nitrogen should be split across the rainy season, and phosphate once.
- Trial rates in the manual were 550 kg potassium oxide and 300 kg nitrogen per hectare. Fertilised plants can yield up to 10 times more than unfertilised plants.

## Pests and diseases

- Banana weevil: use traps (one per 20-30 plants, checked daily early in the morning and renewed every 1-2 weeks), and chop harvested pseudostems and spread them as mulch, because leaving them standing lets weevils multiply.
- Nematodes attack the roots. Many older chemicals listed in the manual are hazardous or no longer allowed, so ask an extension officer which products are approved today.
- Black sigatoka (a leaf disease) can cause yield losses of 30-50%. Fungicides are used against it, and IITA supplies resistant cooking bananas such as Fougamou 1, Bom, Gia Hui, Foulah 4 and Nzizi.

## Propping, thinning and harvest

- Support bearing plants with one or two bamboo props, crossed to form a fork under the bunch.
- Thin all suckers except the tallest one, and repeat about a month later.
- Harvest when one or two fingertips of the first hand start to turn yellow. The bunch is typically 3-4 months old and ripens within a week.
- After harvest, chop the pseudostem and leaves and spread them as mulch for the next crop.

## Replanting

After several crops the corm rises above the soil ("high mat"), which dries the roots and causes tip-over, so mulch helps. At the start of a fallow, destroy the old plants and rest the land for at least 2-3 years with trees or a legume cover crop.

# Growing Rice in Nigeria

Rice is grown in several ways. The most common is rain-fed lowland rice, which is nearly half of the rice area. Others are rain-fed upland rice, irrigated rice, deep-water rice and mangrove swamp rice. Rice needs about 1,200-1,600 mm of evenly spread rain, or a dependable water supply.

## Choosing the field

- Lowland rice does well on heavy, flat land (40-60% clay) with slightly acidic to neutral soil (pH 5.5-7.0), such as fadama edges and irrigation schemes where water lasts about 4.5-5 months.
- Upland rice is grown on fertile soil with rainfall above about 700 mm, but it is at higher risk from drought.
- Two harrowings are enough. Ridging is not needed.

## When to plant

- Forest zone: mid-March to mid-April, after 2-3 good rains.
- Savanna: mid-May to June. IITA gives the first two weeks of June for the Guinea savannas and the third to fourth week of June for the Sudan savanna, and late May to early June for lowland rice.
- An off-season crop with irrigation is also possible. See the planting calendar document.

## Varieties and seed

- Improved varieties named by IITA and NAERLS include FARO 44, FARO 52, FARO 59, FARO 61 and, for upland, FARO 55 (NERICA 1) and FARO 56 (NERICA 2). They mature earlier and compete with weeds better than many local varieties.
- Use certified seed with germination of at least 80%.

## Seed rate and spacing

- Lowland, drilled: 50-60 kg of seed per hectare, in rows 20-30 cm apart.
- Upland, dibbled (holes): 40-50 kg per hectare, with 5-6 seeds per hole at 20 by 20 cm or 30 by 30 cm. Thin to 3-4 seedlings 2-3 weeks after sowing. NAERLS gives 25 by 25 cm (160,000 stands per hectare) with 4-5 seeds per hole.
- Transplanted rice: 2-3 seedlings per hill, 3-4 cm deep, at 20 by 20 cm or 30 by 30 cm. Transplant when the seedlings are about 21 days old.
- Sow 2-4 cm deep and not deeper than 5 cm.

## Fertiliser

- Lowland rice (IITA): 60-80 kg nitrogen, 30 kg phosphorus (P2O5) and 30 kg potassium (K2O) per hectare. At sowing, about 4 bags of NPK 15:15:15. At 6-8 weeks, 2-3 bags of urea buried between rows.
- Upland rice (IITA): 60-80 kg nitrogen with 30 kg each of phosphorus and potassium. Use 60 kg nitrogen after a 1-2 year fallow and 80 kg on older, more frequently cropped soils.
- NAERLS gives nitrogen-phosphorus-potassium rates by zone for upland rice: 70-50-40 for the Sahel and Northern Guinea savanna, 60-30-30 for the Southern Guinea savanna, and 80-30-30 for forest and shallow swamp.
- Apply after enough rain, about 10 cm from the plant base, and mix into the soil. Base the rates on a soil test if you can.

## Water

- Keep 3-5 cm of standing water when applying fertiliser. Drain first if the water is deeper than 10 cm to avoid losing nitrogen.
- Otherwise keep the field evenly flooded to about 10 cm in lowland rice.
- Water shortage at tillering, panicle formation and flowering cuts the yield sharply. Drain the field 10-15 days before harvest.

## Weed control

- Weed by hand at 2-3 weeks and again at 5-6 weeks after sowing. A third weeding may be needed.
- Herbicides are named in the guides (pendimethalin, oxadiazon, propanil mixtures). Read the label, wear protective gear, and ask an extension officer. Herbicides work poorly on badly prepared land.

## Pests and diseases

- Stem borers and armyworms: burn stubble after harvest, or flood the field for a week. Insecticides are named in the guides.
- Birds: scare them for two weeks after planting and again from heading to harvest.
- Rodents: keep a 2-3 m border free of grass.
- Striga: use clean seed and tools and rotate with soybean or groundnut.
- Blast is the most serious disease. Plant resistant varieties, avoid dense nurseries and too much nitrogen, and use split nitrogen doses. Losses can reach 60-100% in favourable conditions. See the Pest Guide entry for rice blast.
- Brown leaf spot: treat seed and burn crop residues.

## Harvest and storage

- Harvest when 80-85% of the grains are straw-coloured, about 30-45 days after flowering.
- Cut stems 10-20 cm above the ground, and dry the bundles before threshing.
- Dry paddy to 12-14% moisture, on mats and not on bare ground, and store in clean, dry, rodent-proof containers.

## Yields

- Farmers' average yields are about 2-3 tonnes per hectare for lowland and 1-1.5 tonnes for upland rice.
- With improved varieties and good practices: lowland 5-8 tonnes per hectare, upland 2.5-6 tonnes per hectare.

# Growing Sorghum and Pearl Millet in Nigeria

Sorghum and pearl millet are the main cereals of the dry northern zones (the Sudan and Sahel savannas), because they cope with low and uneven rainfall better than maize. The guidance below comes from two handbooks written for north-east Nigeria (Borno and Adamawa), so dates and varieties fit that area best.

## Sorghum

**When to plant.** Plant once the soil has enough moisture. In southern Adamawa this is from the end of May, and in northern Borno from the end of June to early July.

**Varieties.** Examples from the handbook:
- SAMSORG 14: Sudan savanna, early (110-120 days), resists leaf diseases.
- SAMSORG 40, 41, 49: Sudan and Sahel savanna, early (75-90 days), tolerate Striga.
- SAMSORG 45 and 46: Sudan and Sahel savanna, 85-95 days, high yielding.
- SAMSORG 17: Guinea savannas, late (160-180 days), tolerates Striga.

**Seed and spacing.** Use 8-10 kg of seed per hectare for a sole crop, treated with a seed dressing. Space plants 75 cm between rows and 25-30 cm within rows, with 3-5 seeds per hole about 5 cm deep. Thin to 2 plants per hill two weeks after sowing.

**Fertiliser.** A general recommendation is 200 kg per hectare of NPK 15:15:15 (4 bags) at planting and 100 kg of urea (2 bags) before flowering, when the soil is moist. Farmyard manure at 2-5 t per hectare can be worked in at land preparation. Micro-dosing is a lower-cost alternative: bury two bottle-cork measures of NPK in a 5 cm hole 5-10 days after emergence, and add one cork of urea 4-5 weeks after sowing.

**Weeds and Striga.** The first six weeks are the critical time, so weed 2-3 times at 3-4 week intervals. For Striga (witchweed), rotate with cotton, groundnut, cowpea or pigeon pea, hand-pull plants before they flower, and grow tolerant varieties. No sorghum variety is completely resistant, and Striga seed can stay alive in the soil for 15-20 years.

**Pests and diseases.**
- Stalk borer: burn or plough under old stalks and stubble, and sow early.
- Sorghum midge: plant early and at the same time as neighbours.
- Head bugs: open-panicle varieties help.
- Smut: treat seed with a fungicide dressing, burn smut galls before spores spread, and do not grow sorghum in the same field more than once every 4 years.
- Anthracnose: use resistant varieties.

**Harvest and storage.** Early varieties are ready around October and medium ones around November in Borno. The crop is mature when leaves turn yellow to brown and the grain tip is brownish black. Harvest promptly, since some varieties shatter. Dry grain to below 12% moisture (it should crack when bitten) and store it in PICS or triple-layer bags. About 35% of grain can be lost to insects in storage.

## Pearl millet

**When to plant.** Plant as soon as the rains are established, and avoid dry planting because early rains are unreliable and seeds may die. Early planting helps the crop escape disease and insects. Dressing seed with an approved chemical gives about 25% more yield than undressed seed.

**Varieties.** SOSAT-C88 and Super SOSAT resist downy mildew. Sudan 9702 is medium maturing and drought tolerant. Jirani is early and drought resistant but is susceptible to downy mildew.

**Spacing.** Use 75 cm between rows and 50 cm within rows. On very poor, dry soils use wider spacing (75 x 100 cm). Sow 5-8 seeds per hill and thin to 2 plants per stand after good rain.

**Fertiliser.** Per hectare: 80 kg N, 30 kg P2O5 and 30 kg K2O in the Sahel, and 60 kg N, 30 kg P and 30 kg K in the Sudan and northern Guinea savannas. As a simple blanket option, use 3-4 bags of NPK 15:15:15 at planting or two weeks after, then 2 bags of urea 4-6 weeks after planting. Manure or compost (3-6 t per hectare) can be added before ridging.

**Weeds.** Hand-weed at 3 and 6 weeks after sowing. Hand-pull Striga before it flowers, and rotate with cotton, groundnut, cowpea or pigeon pea.

**Pests and diseases.** Downy mildew is the most important disease, so choose resistant varieties and burn or compost crop residues. Stem borers, head miners, midges and bugs also attack the crop. Birds can cause severe damage, so scare them.

**Harvest and storage.** Harvest is typically in August to September. Millet is mature when leaves turn yellow to brown and the grain tips are brownish black. Dry grain to 12-13% moisture or less and store it in a well-ventilated place. Threshing before bagging makes insect control easier.

# Growing Soybean in Nigeria

Soybean is a legume that fixes its own nitrogen and is a good rotation crop with maize and sorghum. The guidance below comes from IITA's guide for northern Nigeria.

## When to plant

Wait until the rains are well established, and do not plant early.

| Zone | Planting time |
|---|---|
| Moist savanna and Southern Guinea savanna | Early June to early July |
| Northern Guinea to Sudan savanna | Mid-June to early July |
| Sudan savanna | First two weeks of July |

## Varieties

- Late and medium types, for the Guinea savannas: TGX 1448-2E (late maturing, high yield), TGX 1951-3F (about 2.5 t per hectare) and TGX 1904-6F (1.5-2 t per hectare).
- Early types, for the Sudan savanna: TGX 1987-62F (about 2.1 t per hectare), TGX 1987-10F and TGX 1835-10E (1.5-2 t per hectare). Early and extra-early varieties are strongly recommended for the Sudan savanna.

## Seed

- Use 50-70 kg of seed per hectare, no more than 12 months old, with at least 85% germination.
- Dress the seed with a recommended seed dressing.
- Inoculate seed with a rhizobium inoculant (such as NoduMax) at about 10 g per kg of seed. Mix in the shade and plant the same day. This lets the crop fix its own nitrogen.

## Spacing and sowing

Sow 3-4 seeds per hole, 75 cm between rows and 10 cm between stands, 2-5 cm deep. Alternatively drill the seed in rows 50-75 cm apart. Early varieties do well at 50 cm between rows and 5-10 cm within rows.

## Fertiliser

Apply about 30 kg of phosphorus per hectare as single superphosphate (about 3 bags of 50 kg), with some NPK 15:15:15. Add nitrogen and potassium only where a shortage is clear. Work the fertiliser in at harrowing.

## Weed control

Hand-weed at 2 weeks and again at 5-6 weeks after planting, and do not weed straight after rain. Herbicides are available for before planting, just after planting and after emergence. Ask an extension officer which to use, and follow the label.

## Pests and diseases

- Pod-sucking bugs after flowering can be controlled with an approved insecticide spray.
- Rust can cause up to 80% yield loss. Bacterial pustule, Phytophthora root rot and frogeye leaf spot are also important. Plant resistant varieties, use well-drained land, dress the seed, and rotate with maize.
- For virus diseases, use certified seed, remove infected plants, clear weeds around the field and control the insects that spread the viruses.

## Harvest and yield

- The crop is ready 3-4 months after planting. Harvest when about 85% of pods are brown (80% for shattering varieties).
- Cut at ground level, stack loosely on a tarpaulin, dry in the open for two weeks and then thresh.
- With good practices and suitable varieties, yields of 2,500-3,000 kg per hectare are possible.

## Storage

Store at 10% moisture or less (12% for 6-12 months of storage), in bags on racks in the shade or a cool room.

# Growing Sweet Potato in Nigeria

Sweet potato is a short-duration root crop that is ready in about 3.5-5 months. No single Nigerian extension guide for it was found, so the guidance below combines two Nigerian research papers and an IITA paper on the weevil. Ask an extension officer for local details such as planting dates and fertiliser rates.

## Climate and soil

- Temperature of 20-28°C and rainfall of 500-1,250 mm suit the crop.
- The best soil is well-drained sandy loam with plenty of organic matter, at a pH of about 5.8-6.0.
- Soil about 20 cm deep is enough.

## Planting material

- Plant vine cuttings, not seed. Cuttings in one Nigerian trial were about 30 cm long with at least 4-5 nodes (buds), cut early in the morning and planted straight away.
- The growing tips (apical cuttings) gave the best establishment and yield, about 3.0 t per hectare against 2.4 t for cuttings from the base of the vine.

## Planting method and spacing

Plant on mounds, ridges, flat ground or beds. Spacing depends on the system:
- 100 x 30 cm gives about 33,000 plants per hectare.
- 45 x 45 cm gives about 49,000 plants per hectare.
- 30 x 60 cm gives about 55,000 plants per hectare.
- 60 x 120 cm gives about 13,000 plants per hectare.

## Soil fertility

The papers give no fertiliser rates. They recommend improved fallow, rotation with legumes and manure to improve poor soils.

## Weed control

The crop needs little weeding because the vines soon cover the ground. Keep the field weed-free until about six weeks after planting, and take care not to cover the vines with soil when weeding.

## Pests and diseases

- Sweet potato weevils (Cylas) are described as the most destructive insect pests of sweet potato, with tuber losses of up to 90%. Because the grubs feed hidden inside the tuber, chemicals work poorly and are costly.
- IITA research found that planting early, harvesting early and re-ridging (earthing up) reduce weevil damage greatly. In one trial, late-planted and not re-ridged crops had 42-58% unmarketable tubers of a susceptible variety, while early-planted, early-harvested and re-ridged crops had negligible damage.
- Weevil-resistant varieties help further.
- Sweet potato virus disease is also a problem.

## Harvest and yield

The crop matures in about 3.5-5 months. In the Nigerian trial, yields were 2-3 t per hectare for the varieties and conditions tested. The Nigerian review gives no per-hectare yield figure for farmers' fields.

# Growing Tomato in Nigeria

Tomato is a major vegetable crop in Nigeria. It is started in a nursery and moved to the field as young plants (transplanting). The guidance below comes from the National Horticultural Research Institute (NIHORT).

## Climate

- Tomato grows best in a cool, dry climate of about 21-25°C.
- In the north, the variety Roma VF is suited to dry-season planting, and irrigation may be needed.
- NIHORT's guide does not give a month-by-month planting calendar.

## Varieties

- Ife No. 1 and NHLe158-3 suit both north and south.
- Ibadan Local suits the humid rainforest zone.
- NHLy11 suits backyard gardens.

## Nursery

- Make seedbeds about 50 cm wide.
- Sow in rows 5 cm apart and cover thinly with fine soil. Seeds sprout in 3-5 days.
- One week before transplanting, harden the seedlings by gradually removing the shade.
- Seedlings are ready 4-5 weeks after sowing, when about 10 cm tall.

## Transplanting and spacing

- Space plants 50 cm within rows and 75 cm between rows. That is about 26,700 plants per hectare.

## Fertiliser

- Apply organic manure two weeks before transplanting.
- Apply NPK 15:15:15 at 200-250 kg per hectare in two equal doses by the ring method: the first about 3 weeks after transplanting and the second when fruit starts to set.

## Staking

- Stake with canes at least 1 m tall, preferably bamboo.
- When tomato is sown in the rainy season, stake at the peak of the rains.

## Weed control

- Keep the field free of weeds from planting until early fruit set.
- Weed at 3-5 weeks after transplanting, then at least twice more before harvest ends.

## Pests and diseases

- Insect pests are controlled with an insecticide sprayed weekly according to its label. Spray more or less often depending on pest pressure.
- Ask an extension officer to confirm which products to use and how much.

See the Pest Guide entries for late blight, tomato leafminer (Tuta absoluta) and whitefly.

## Harvest and yield

- The crop is ready 12-18 weeks after sowing. It can stay in the field for 17-23 weeks under good care.
- For the fresh market or long transport, harvest when fruits are half ripe or just starting to change colour. Processing tomatoes must be fully ripe.
- Harvest at least once a week.
- With intensive management yields of 20-40 tonnes per hectare are possible. In northern Nigeria they reach up to 20 tonnes per hectare, and dry-season irrigation can raise yields.

# Growing Yam in Nigeria

Yam is a main crop of the southern Guinea savanna and the derived savanna. The only detailed guide found is IITA's manual on growing seed yam from small pieces (minisetts). The same planting and care steps apply to growing yam generally, but the manual gives no tonnage yields for ware yam (yam for eating).

## Planting material: the minisett method

Traditional seed yam gives about 5 tubers for each one planted. The minisett method gives up to 30.

1. Choose healthy mother tubers of 800-1,000 g that have come out of dormancy.
2. Cut them into rings 2-5 cm thick, then cut each ring into pieces (minisetts) of 25-100 g, keeping some peel on each piece so it can sprout.
3. Treat the cut pieces with an approved fungicide and insecticide (a dip for 5-10 minutes, or shaking with a powder). Wood ash is an alternative.
4. Dry the pieces in a shaded, airy place for 12-24 hours before planting.

The target seed tuber size is 150-300 g, and bigger minisetts give bigger seed yams.

## When to plant

Plant directly only when the rainy season is fully established, unless you can irrigate. You can also pre-sprout minisetts in a nursery, where sprouts appear 2-4 weeks after planting, and transplant when the sprouts are short and the rains are steady.

## Ridges, spacing and staking

- Plant on ridges or mounds, about 10 cm deep, with the skin side pressed firmly against the soil.
- Single rows: 25 x 100 cm, about 40,000 plants per hectare. Double rows on ridges 1 m apart with 25 cm between plants give about 80,000 plants per hectare, with smaller seed yams.
- Maize can be planted at the same time as an intercrop.
- Stake the vines with 1-2 m stakes, using pyramid staking or a trellis of poles and rope.

## Fertiliser

Apply NPK 20:10:10 or 15:15:15 at 400-500 kg per hectare in bands on both sides of the ridge, at 8-12 weeks after planting (4-8 weeks for pre-sprouted setts).

## Weed control

Keep the field clean, especially in the first 8-12 weeks, until the vines cover the ground at about 12 weeks. Earth up exposed setts, roots and tubers at the second or third weeding.

## Pests and diseases

- Start with disease-free setts and keep the field weed-free.
- Remove diseased plants early and control insect vectors.
- Virus signs are mosaic, vein banding, mottling, leaf distortion and stunting.
- Avoid places where water collects, because they favour nematodes.
- Nematodes, mealybugs, scale insects and crickets are the main pests.

See the Pest Guide entry for yam anthracnose.

## Harvest, curing and storage

- Seed yams are ready 5-6 months after planting, when the leaves yellow and the vines dry.
- Dig carefully to avoid cuts and bruises, avoid digging in wet soil, and move tubers into the shade right away to prevent sunscald.
- Cure by piling the tubers and covering them for 2-3 days.
- Store in a shaded, well-ventilated, secure place. Inspect often, remove rotting tubers and early sprouts, and store each variety separately.

# Cassava Bacterial Blight (Disease)

Affects: Cassava. Severity: High.

## What it is

Cassava bacterial blight is caused by bacteria that enter through leaves and wounds and move inside the plant. It spreads by rain splash, dirty cutting tools and infected cuttings, and is more common in the drier savanna areas. Root losses can be very high in susceptible varieties.

## Symptoms

- Small, dark, water-soaked angular spots on leaves, sometimes with a yellow halo
- Spots that join into dry brown patches, with leaves that curl and wither
- Wilting leaf stalks
- Shoot tips that die back
- It can look like drought damage, so it is easy to mistake

## Treatment and control

- There is no cure
- After harvest, remove and burn or bury infected plant debris
- Control weeds, which can host the bacteria
- Rotate with a non-host crop or leave the field fallow for a season
- Replant only with cuttings from healthy fields and clean your cutting tools between plants

## Prevention

- Use disease-free cuttings. This is the most important step
- Choose tolerant varieties. Resistance is partial, so ask your extension office what works in your area
- Never take cuttings from a field that had blight
- Clean or burn debris between seasons

# Cassava Mealybug (Pest)

Affects: Cassava. Severity: High.

## What it is

The cassava mealybug is a tiny soft insect covered in white waxy fluff. It sucks sap from shoot tips and young leaves and builds up fastest in the dry season. A small imported wasp now keeps it under control in most places, but outbreaks can still cause heavy root loss.

## Symptoms

- White cottony clusters on shoot tips, young leaves and stem joints
- Shoot tips that stay short and bunch together
- Leaves that curl, yellow and drop, and stems that twist or die back
- Sticky honeydew on leaves with black sooty mould
- Ants moving on the plant to tend the insects

## Treatment and control

- Cut off infested shoot tips and burn or bury them. Do not leave them in the field or carry them to other farms

- Use chemicals only as a last resort and follow the product label

## Prevention

- Plant clean cuttings from healthy plants and inspect them first
- Do not share or sell cuttings from infested fields
- Remove weeds and clean your tools
- Check your field more often in the dry season

# Erosion Control and Mulching on Nigerian Farms

**Source note.** The erosion advice comes mainly from one survey of 120 farmers in Ikeduru, Imo State (south-east Nigeria), so it shows what local farmers use, not a tested recommendation for every zone. Ask an extension officer about the best method for your slope and soil.

## Why it matters

- Water erosion, wind erosion and gully erosion are all present in Nigeria. In the Imo survey water erosion was the most common, then wind and gullies.
- Erosion removes topsoil and nutrients, lowers crop yields, cuts income and can destroy farmland and houses.
- The national fertiliser review notes that heavy rain in the forest and coastal zones has badly leached soils and caused severe erosion in places.

## Practices that make erosion worse

The Imo authors name bush burning, clean weeding (leaving the soil bare), overgrazing and deforestation. Reducing these helps.

## Measures farmers use

- **Mulching.** Cover the soil with grass, crop residues or leaves. It protects the surface from raindrops, reduces weeding and keeps moisture in. IITA recommends complete mulch cover in plantain fields and NIHORT recommends grass mulch for pineapple.
- **Cover crops.** Plants such as legumes that cover the soil between main crops.
- **Tree windbreaks and shade trees.** Rows of trees across the wind.
- **Contour ridges.** Make ridges across the slope instead of up and down. In the survey, farmers adopted this only moderately.
- **Terracing.** Recommended for steep land with proper drainage channels, but it needs a lot of labour and money, so it is hard for smallholders.
- **Other measures** the paper lists: improved crop varieties, minimum tillage, intercropping, organic manure, and adjusting planting time.

## Practical tips

- On sloping land, plant across the slope and keep ground cover in the rainy season.
- Do not burn the whole field. Mulch or incorporate residues instead.
- Keep drainage channels clear so runoff does not form gullies.
- Act on gullies early, because small ones grow quickly. For large gullies ask the local agricultural office for help.

# Using Fertiliser (NPK, Urea and Others) on Nigerian Crops

## The basics

- The three main nutrients are nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P) and potassium (K). An NPK label such as 15:15:15 or 20:10:10 shows the percentage of each.
- Common products in Nigeria: urea (46% N), CAN (calcium ammonium nitrate, a nitrogen fertiliser), single superphosphate or SSP (about 19% P2O5, a phosphorus fertiliser), TSP (triple superphosphate), and MOP (muriate of potash, about 60% K2O, a potassium fertiliser).
- Most farmers use only NPK 15:15:15, NPK 20:10:10 and urea. These have no micronutrients, which some soils lack.
- Nigeria's recommendations are published by the Federal Ministry of Agriculture (FMARD). The national average use is under 20 kg of nutrient per hectare, so many fields get far less than they need.

## General rules

- **Split nitrogen.** Nitrogen washes away easily, so apply it in two or three parts instead of all at once. Example from savanna rice research: 120 kg N per hectare split as 50 kg at early tillering, 50 kg at panicle initiation and 20 kg at booting. Phosphorus and potassium went on before sowing.
- **Place it carefully.** Do not put fertiliser against the stem. For cassava the recommendation is a ring 10 cm from the plant at about 6 cm depth, and for yam about 15 cm from the vine base, at 3-5 cm depth.
- **Apply when the soil is moist,** at planting or a few weeks after, as the crop table below shows.
- **Match the rate to the field.** Rates in research are averages. A soil test, or advice from an extension officer, fits the rate to your field.
- **Do not overdo it.** In the rice trial, nitrogen above 120 kg per hectare gave no significant gain, and phosphorus and potassium above 50 kg per hectare lowered yield.
- **Organic matter helps fertiliser work better.** See the manure and compost document.

## Recommended rates (national recommendations, per hectare)

These are the FMARD recommendations quoted in the 2018 AfricaFertilizer review. They are for typical yields, so adjust for your soil.

| Crop | Recommendation | Timing |
|---|---|---|
| Maize, northern zones (7-8 t/ha target) | About 300 kg NPK 20:10:10 at planting or 2-3 weeks after planting, plus about 125 kg urea at 5-6 weeks. Also SSP and MOP as the review lists. | Split |
| Maize, southern Guinea savanna (4-5 t/ha) | About 220 kg urea (or 385 kg CAN), split half at 2-3 weeks and half at 5-6 weeks, with SSP (about 278 kg) and MOP (about 50 kg). | Split |
| Rice, upland | NPK 15:15:15 as basal and urea as top dressing. Overall about 275-325 kg per hectare of product. | Planting, 1-2 weeks, 6 weeks |
| Rice, lowland | Same products, about 350-450 kg per hectare of product. | Planting and 6-7 weeks |
| Sorghum | About 220 kg NPK 20:10:10 in the northern zones (about 160 kg in the southern Guinea savanna), with SSP and MOP. | Planting, 2-3 and 6-8 weeks |
| Millet | About 300 kg NPK 20:10:10 in the northern zones (about 150 kg elsewhere). | Planting and 3 weeks |
| Cassava | About 196 kg urea or equivalent, with SSP (about 288 kg) and MOP (about 90 kg K2O). Poultry manure at 3 t plus 200 kg NPK 20:10:10 is an organic alternative. | Planting or 8 weeks |
| Yam | About 450-600 kg per hectare of a mixed product (15:15:15 or 20:10:10). Add magnesium, and lime (500 kg per hectare) if soil pH is below 4.5. | 8-9 weeks after planting |
| Cowpea | About 100 kg NPK 20:10:10 in the north, and mostly phosphorus (SSP) and potassium in the Guinea savanna and forest zones. | Planting or 2 weeks |
| Groundnut | SSP (about 300 kg) with MOP (about 42 kg). Supply sulphur if using a different phosphorus source. Use borax (0.6 kg per hectare) where boron is short. | Planting or 2 weeks |
| Soybean | About 350 kg per hectare of mixed NPK 15:15:15 and SSP in the north, and SSP-based mixtures with a little nitrogen elsewhere. | Planting or 2 weeks |

The review's rice table is ambiguous about some product quantities, and it had no rates for tomato, cocoa, oil palm or cotton. For those, use the figures in the crop documents and ask an extension officer.

## Safety

Wear gloves, keep fertiliser away from water sources and children, and store bags dry and sealed.

# Organic Manure and Compost for Nigerian Farms

**Source note.** The composting method is from an FAO home-garden leaflet, so quantities are for small plots, and no Nigerian bulletin with manure rates was found apart from one cassava recommendation. Ask an extension officer for local rates.

## Why add organic matter

Nigerian research links the decline in savanna soil fertility to long-term fertiliser use with little or no organic matter. Manure and compost feed the crop slowly, hold moisture and improve the soil, and they also work well together with fertiliser.

## Sources of organic matter

- Animal manure, such as cattle, goat or poultry droppings.
- Crop residues, leaves, grass cuttings and kitchen peelings. Weeds are fine only if they have no seeds.
- Wood ash in small amounts.
- Legumes in rotation, and mulch laid on the soil surface.
- One recommended cassava combination is 3 t of poultry manure plus 200 kg of NPK 20:10:10 per hectare.
- For pepper, one NAERLS bulletin suggests 25-37.5 t per hectare a year of manure or compost.

## How to make compost (FAO method)

1. **Gather materials.** Chop or shred plant matter, and remove large woody pieces.
2. **Choose the site.** Shade, flat ground, and no waterlogging. Build on the surface, not in a pit, so air can move around it.
3. **Layer.** Put the coarsest material at the bottom for drainage. Then alternate soft, cut-up material with fibrous material in layers 15-20 cm thick. Add a thin layer (2-3 cm) of soil to bring in microorganisms.
4. **Size.** Keep the heap about 1-1.5 m high and wide, so you can walk around it. Push in vertical bamboo poles or bunches of dry grass about 1 m apart to let air in.
5. **Cover.** In hot, dry weather or heavy rain, cover the heap with banana leaves or similar material.
6. **Keep it moist.** About 45-65% humidity. If it is too wet, turn it or add dry material.
7. **Turn.** The heap can heat to 60-80°C, which kills many pests and weed seeds. Turn it after about 15 days, moving the bottom to the top, and again after about five weeks, adding water if it is dry.
8. **Check readiness.** After about three months, compost should be dark brown, crumbly and earthy-smelling. Sieve out lumps that have not broken down.

## Using compost

- Apply just before planting and mix it into the top layer of soil.
- Compost that is not fully decomposed can tie up nitrogen, so add extra nitrogen (manure or fertiliser) if you use it.
- The FAO leaflet suggests roughly one large sack of finished compost for about 10 square metres in a garden.

# Soil Basics and Soil Testing for Nigerian Farms

**Source note.** The soil facts come from Nigerian research on savanna soils and a national fertiliser review. The soil-sampling steps come from a US extension leaflet, because no Nigerian farmer-level sampling guide was found. The method is general, but ask a local soil laboratory, university or extension office how they want samples prepared.

## What Nigerian soils often lack

- Nigerian research on savanna rice soils found them slightly acid (pH 6-7) with low nitrogen, low available phosphorus, low exchangeable bases and low organic carbon.
- In that research, nitrogen was the most limiting nutrient, then phosphorus, then potassium. Leaving out nitrogen cut yield by 39%, phosphorus by 30-34% and potassium by 17-27%. With no nitrogen, phosphorus or potassium at all, yields fell by about 75%.
- In the forest and coastal zones, heavy rain has leached many soils badly and caused severe erosion in places.
- The national fertiliser review says Nigerian farmers use under 20 kg of nutrient per hectare on average, against a world average of about 100 kg.
- The same research blames long-term fertiliser use with little or no organic matter for declining fertility in the savanna. Adding manure, compost or crop residues helps.
- Some crops have specific needs: yam soils with pH below 4.5 need lime and magnesium, and groundnut in the savanna can lack boron and sulphur.

## Why soil testing helps

A soil test measures the nutrients in a field and guides what to buy and how much to apply. The Nigerian authors point out that farmers often lack knowledge of fertiliser type, rate, timing and method, and say rates should fit each field instead of one fixed rate everywhere.

## How to take a soil sample

1. Use a soil auger or probe if you can. A clean spade and clean bucket also work.
2. Sample one uniform area at a time. If parts of the field differ (colour, slope, past crops, previously fertilised or limed), sample them separately.
3. Take at least 15-20 small samples in a zigzag across the area, each at the same depth. The US leaflet uses about 15 cm (6 inches) for cropland, and the Nigerian trials sampled 0-15 cm.
4. Avoid unusual spots such as old fence lines, areas near trees, fertiliser or lime spills, and the edges of paths. In row crops, sample between the rows, away from fertiliser bands. Scrape off surface residue first.
5. Mix all the samples in the bucket to make one composite sample for that area.
6. Break up clods and air-dry the sample at room temperature. Do not use heat from a stove or fire.
7. Put the sample in a clean container, label it with the field name, and send it to a soil laboratory with a sketch of the sampled area.

## When to sample

Sample before land preparation and before applying fertiliser or manure. The US leaflet suggests testing each field about once every three years or once per rotation.

## Using the result

The laboratory report will guide lime and fertiliser decisions. If you cannot get a test, ask an extension officer for the recommended fertiliser rate for your crop and zone (see the fertiliser document).

# Choosing Seed and Improved Varieties

**Source note.** No single Nigerian guide on choosing seed was found. This document combines a newspaper report on the National Agricultural Seeds Council (NASC), the Nigerian Seed Portal, and two maize studies. The crop documents name improved varieties for each crop.

## Why variety matters

Good seed and a suitable variety shape yield before any fertiliser is applied. Pick a variety for your zone, your rainfall and the length of the growing season. Look at maturity (days to harvest), resistance to local diseases and pests, and yield potential.

## Where to find information

- **Nigerian Seed Portal** (seedportal.org.ng) lists improved varieties with their crop, release year, notable traits (disease resistance, maturity, grain or fruit qualities) and yield potential in tonnes per hectare. It also points to registered seed companies. It does not list agro-ecological zones, so check suitability with an extension officer.
- **Research institutes** such as IITA, NIHORT, NCRI, CRIN and the universities breed and release varieties. Crop documents in this knowledge base name examples (for example cassava, cowpea, groundnut, cocoa and pineapple).

## Buying genuine seed

- NASC is the body that checks seed quality in Nigeria. A newspaper report says it runs education campaigns about adulterated and fake seed and unscrupulous seed merchants, and that the Seed Act of 1992 punishes violators. It tests varieties in plots at Hadejia (Jigawa), Dan Hassan (Kano) and Jega (Kebbi), and seed lots that fail minimum standards are not to be sold as seed.
- The report says more than 80 seed companies now operate in Nigeria, up from two.
- The report does not list how to spot a fake. As a general rule, buy from a registered seed company or an agro-dealer who can show where the seed came from, and ask your extension officer which sellers are reliable.
- For crops planted from saved seed or cuttings, use healthy, clean material, and see the crop documents for planting-material advice (for example, cassava stems from disease-free plants).

## Matching variety to rainfall

Research by IITA with farmers in Borno and Kano states found that early and extra-early drought-tolerant maize varieties suited dry northern conditions. In Borno, farmers liked an early variety that matures in late September so they could harvest and sell before the drier period. In resource-poor Kano farms, farmers preferred extra-early varieties to bridge the hungry months of August and September, and for them earliness mattered more than yield.

A national survey of 1,370 rural households found that farmers who grow drought-tolerant maize also tend to use inorganic fertiliser, intercropping and manure, with the strongest link to manure. The survey does not report yield effects.

## Practical checklist

1. Decide what you need: early harvest, drought tolerance, disease resistance, or a specific market quality.
2. Check the variety's maturity and traits on the Seed Portal or with an extension officer.
3. Buy from a registered source, and keep the receipt and the seed bag label.
4. Plant at the right time and spacing for that variety (see the crop and planting calendar documents).

# Integrated Pest Management and Safe Pesticide Use

**Source note.** The safety facts come from a 2025 survey of Kano tomato farmers. The IPM examples come from crop guides used elsewhere in this knowledge base. This document gives no product names or doses. Follow the product label and ask an extension officer which products are approved today.

## What IPM means

Integrated pest management (IPM) means using several methods together, and using chemicals only when needed. The examples below are all drawn from Nigerian crop guides.

## Methods to use first

- **Prevention and clean farming.** Use clean planting material, rotate crops (do not plant pepper where pepper or tomato grew last year), keep the field clean, and remove infested plant parts. Cashew guides advise removing infested branches, and plantain guides advise chopping harvested pseudostems so weevils do not breed.
- **Timing.** For sweet potato weevil, IITA found that planting early, harvesting early and re-ridging (earthing up) cut damage greatly. In one trial, late-planted crops without re-ridging had 42-58% unmarketable tubers of a susceptible variety, while early-planted, re-ridged crops had almost none.
- **Resistant or tolerant varieties.** Examples include blast-tolerant rice, rosette-resistant groundnut and mosaic-resistant cassava. See the crop and pest documents.
- **Hand-picking and traps.** Hornworms on pepper can be hand-picked in small fields, stem girdlers on cashew can be picked and crushed, and banana weevil can be trapped (one trap per 20-30 plants, checked daily and renewed every 1-2 weeks).
- **Natural enemies.** Cashew guides advise not spraying the nests of weaver ants and spiders, which prey on mirids. IITA has also tested a small parasitic wasp against the legume pod borer.
- **Scouting.** Walk the field regularly and check the plants, so you can act early. Check the pest guide for what each pest looks like.

## When chemicals are used

- Spray only when the pest is present and damaging, and choose a product approved for that crop and pest.
- Be wary of old chemicals. Several older bulletins name products that are now banned or restricted, so these are deliberately left out of this knowledge base.
- A Nigerian survey found about 70% of farmers say ineffective or counterfeit pesticides are common. About 63% bought from agro-dealers and 35% from open markets. Buy sealed, labelled products from a trusted dealer.
- About 12% of surveyed farmers used Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt), a biological insecticide, but 53% thought IPM was less effective than chemical sprays. The authors suggest farmer field schools and participatory training.

## Staying safe

- **Protective equipment.** In the survey most farmers wore a face mask (84%), but fewer used rubber boots (62%), protective clothing (36%) or goggles (16%). Wear gloves, a face covering, goggles, protective clothing and boots when mixing and spraying.
- **Read the label.** 83% of farmers did not know what a pre-harvest interval means. It is the number of days to wait between the last spray and harvest. The survey's average wait was about 7.5 days, and about 40% harvested within 5 days of spraying, which is risky for consumers. Always observe the interval on the label.
- **Storage.** About 22% of farmers kept pesticides with food. Keep them in a locked, separate place, away from children, animals, food and water.
- **Disposal.** Many farmers reused empty containers (45%), burned them (15%) or dumped them on the farm (14%). Do not reuse empty containers for food or water. Ask your extension officer or spray-service provider how to dispose of them. Farmers who had disposal training were about 19 percentage points more likely to dispose properly.
- **Never spray near water,** food crops about to be picked, or other people.

## Where to get help

Ask your local agricultural extension officer, an ADP office or your state ministry of agriculture about approved products and training.

# Post-harvest Handling and Storage

**Source note.** The hermetic-bag advice comes from a Purdue extension leaflet and a Nigerian study. Moisture targets and crop details are drawn from the crop documents (NAERLS, IITA, NIHORT and research papers). Moisture levels differ slightly by source, so use the crop documents for each crop.

## Principles

1. **Harvest at the right stage,** and handle gently to avoid bruises and cuts.
2. **Dry properly and fast.** Dry on mats, tarpaulins or raised platforms, never on bare soil.
3. **Clean first.** Remove debris and damaged produce before storing. Debris can puncture bags.
4. **Keep it dry and off the ground.** Use racks or pallets, and a store that is clean, ventilated and leak-proof.
5. **Keep pests out.** Rodents, weevils and moulds cause large losses.

## Target moisture for storage (from the crop documents)

| Crop | Moisture target |
|---|---|
| Maize | 10-12%, and below 12% to avoid mould |
| Rice (paddy) | 12-14% |
| Cowpea | 7-8% (should crack when bitten) |
| Groundnut kernels | Under 10% (pods dried to 8-10%) |
| Soybean | 10% or less (12% for 6-12 months) |
| Sorghum and millet | 12-13% or less |
| Cashew nuts | About 8-9% (dry on a concrete slab for 2-3 days until they rattle) |
| Cocoa beans | About 7% (6-8%) |

## Hermetic (airtight) storage

- A PICS bag is a triple-layer airtight bag with two inner plastic liners and an outer woven bag. It stores grain without chemicals and can be reused. The Purdue leaflet reports a median useful life of about three years for cowpea storage in the Sahel.
- Fill with clean grain, handle carefully when tying and moving, keep bags away from direct sun, heat and rodents, and patch small holes with tape.
- A Nigerian study in the north-west found cowpea farmers using PICS bags had higher food security and lower poverty than non-users, partly because they avoided misused insecticides and storage losses.
- The maize document adds that hermetic bags suit storage beyond six months.

## Special cases

- **Groundnut and maize (aflatoxin).** Pods bagged wet, damaged pods and drying on soil allow mould that produces aflatoxin, a poison. Dry properly and store in clean, dry conditions (see the groundnut document).
- **Yam.** Cure and store in a shaded, well-ventilated place, check often and remove rotting tubers.
- **Onion.** Harvest when necks soften and tops fall over, cure and dry the neck, and store on slatted stacks with good airflow, for up to six months.
- **Tomato and other fresh vegetables.** Fresh produce does not store long. Harvest at the right stage, keep it cool and shaded, and sell or process quickly. The sources used here give no storage figures.
- **Mango.** NIHORT notes that large amounts of fruit are wasted at peak harvest because of poor storage and processing, and has developed juice, puree, flakes and jam.

## Grading and quality

Buyers pay more for clean, dry, uniform produce. Cashew processors want sound nuts not smaller than 5.6 g, and cocoa buyers grade beans by the share of slaty, mouldy and other defective beans (see the cocoa document).

# Water Management and Basic Irrigation

**Source note.** No Nigerian irrigation-scheduling guide for smallholders was found. The crop figures below come from NAERLS and NIHORT bulletins already used in the crop documents, and the national picture comes from a 2018 policy case study. For crops not listed here, ask an extension officer.

## Where irrigation is used

- Small-scale irrigation in Nigeria has mostly used motor pumps, boreholes and tube wells, often in fadama (seasonally flooded valley bottoms), and government programmes have supported these. Rice, maize and vegetables such as tomato, cabbage, okra and pumpkin were the main crops in the FADAMA programme.
- In the north, dry-season irrigation allows onion, tomato, pepper and groundnut to be grown after the rains (see the crop and planting calendar documents).
- The case study says large public schemes often failed because of high running costs, weak maintenance and poor ownership by farmers, while small systems gave better returns.

## How much water some crops need

| Crop | Water guidance | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Pepper | About 600-800 mm per season, roughly 15-20 irrigations by basin or furrow | NAERLS |
| Onion | Every 5-7 days in the field, about 15-20 irrigations (350-550 mm in total). Sensitive to water stress, and waterlogging damages it badly | NAERLS |
| Groundnut (dry season) | Every 7-10 days by furrow or sprinkler | Groundnut document |
| Citrus | In the dry season (November to February), 10 litres at the base of each tree, twice a week | NIHORT |
| Plantain | At least 100 mm of rain a month, well spread. Irrigation is generally not practical for smallholders, so plant in the rains | IITA |

## Good practice

- **Water at the root, not the whole field,** where you can. Basin and furrow irrigation put water along the plants.
- **Water in the early morning or late evening** to cut evaporation (general practice, not stated in the bulletins above).
- **Avoid waterlogging.** Onion and many crops are damaged by standing water. Choose well-drained land and keep drainage channels open.
- **Mulch.** Grass or crop residue on the soil saves moisture and reduces weeds. NIHORT and IITA recommend mulching for pineapple and plantain.
- **Harden seedlings.** Pepper nurseries are watered less for three days before transplanting.
- **Match the water source to the area.** A permanent source (a river, borehole or fadama) is needed for dry-season crops, and NIHORT advises choosing citrus land near a permanent water source.
- **Mind the cost.** Small pumps and boreholes are cheaper to run than large schemes, but fuel, repairs and labour should be planned before planting a dry-season crop.

# Weed Control on Smallholder Farms

**Source note.** Most of this comes from crop-specific guides used elsewhere in this knowledge base. IITA's cassava page is the only source that describes a whole weed-management sequence, and it names no herbicide products or rates, so ask an extension officer for those.

## The key idea: weed early

Weeds compete with the crop for water, light and nutrients, and the early weeks matter most. IITA says cassava is most vulnerable to weeds in the first 10-16 weeks and needs weed control until its canopy closes, which can take up to 20-24 weeks after planting.

## A sequence that works (from IITA's cassava steps)

1. Choose the field and start clearing about two weeks before planting.
2. Slash the vegetation.
3. Clear the land (IITA's page uses herbicide for this step).
4. Plough and ridge.
5. Plant, and apply a pre-emergence herbicide if you use one.
6. After weeds appear, weed by hand and/or with chemicals until the crop shades the ground.

## How often to weed (examples from the crop documents)

- **Pepper:** hand-weed shallowly three times, three weeks apart, starting three weeks after transplanting.
- **Cotton:** weed by hand about every three weeks.
- **Rice (research trial):** hoe weeding at 4 and 8 weeks after sowing.
- **Sweet potato:** keep weed-free until about six weeks after planting. The vines then cover the ground.
- **Plantain:** weed every 6-8 weeks at first, using a full mulch cover as the best control.
- **Cashew:** weed 3-5 times a year and mulch in the first dry season.
- **Onion:** weed early, since onion competes poorly.

## Other methods

- **Mulch.** Grass, crop residues or other plant material suppress weeds and save water (plantain, pineapple, cashew).
- **Careful cultivation.** Take care not to damage roots or cover vines with soil.

## Herbicides

IITA notes that herbicide use may be subject to regulations and permits in some countries, and that products are applied with knapsack sprayers. Always read the label, wear protective clothing, and ask an extension officer which products are approved and suited to your crop. See the safe pesticide document.

## Striga

Striga (witchweed) attacks sorghum, millet, maize and cowpea in the north. See the crop documents for resistant varieties and rotation.

# Coping with Erratic Rainfall and Climate Variability

**Source note.** The forecast details are from NiMet's 2026 seasonal prediction and are examples of what NiMet publishes each year, not predictions for future seasons. Always use the current year's NiMet forecast.

## Do not plant on the first rains

NiMet's advice for 2026 was that early rain in parts of southern Nigeria is not the start of the rainy season. A NiMet official said showers before the official onset, especially after January rain in the south, are usually followed by a break, and farmers who rushed to plant have repeatedly lost money to dry spells that followed. He advised using the onset dates in NiMet's seasonal forecast, which are given for each local government area, because onset and end dates can differ even between the north and south of one state (for example Ogun).

The sorghum and millet document gives the same advice: avoid dry planting where early rains are unreliable, since seeds may die.

## What NiMet's forecast contains

NiMet's yearly seasonal prediction gives expected onset (early, normal, late), cessation (end of rains), length of season and dry-spell warnings, by state or area. The 2026 forecast included, for example, early onset in many states, a late onset in Borno, a longer season in some states, dry spells of more than 15 days in March to May in parts of Oyo and Ogun, dry spells of up to 21 days in June to August in northern and central states, and an August Break that could be severe in Lagos, Ogun, Ekiti and parts of Oyo. Use this as an example of what to look for in the current forecast.

## Ways to reduce the risk

1. **Check the forecast** for your local government area before planting, and wait for the confirmed onset.
2. **Choose varieties to match the season.** Early and extra-early varieties escape late-season dryness. IITA trials in Borno and Kano found early drought-tolerant maize (maturing around late September in Borno) and extra-early varieties suited the dry north. See the seed document.
3. **Stagger planting** if you can, so that a dry spell does not hit the whole crop at the same stage. Plan around the August Break in the south.
4. **Mulch** to hold soil moisture (plantain, pineapple, cashew and other crops all benefit).
5. **Add organic matter** so soil holds more water. A national survey found farmers who grow drought-tolerant maize also tend to use manure, intercropping and fertiliser.
6. **Keep a water source** for dry-season or vital crops (see the irrigation document), and avoid planting water-hungry crops such as plantain where rain is not well spread.
7. **Match the crop to the zone.** Sorghum and millet cope with low and uneven rain better than maize, which is why they dominate the north (see the zone documents).
8. **Spread the risk.** Grow more than one crop, and consider both early and later varieties.
9. **Time field work to the weather:** weed early, apply nitrogen when the soil is moist, and harvest and dry in dry weather.

Points 3, 8 and 9 are general farming practice, not taken from the sources above.

## Where to find forecasts

NiMet publishes the Seasonal Climate Prediction each year (nimet.gov.ng). The extension officer in your ADP can also share it, and the planting calendar document gives typical planting windows to compare with it.
